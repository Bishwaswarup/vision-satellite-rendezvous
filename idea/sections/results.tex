\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

The proposed vision-based rendezvous architecture was evaluated through
six experiments covering numerical dynamics validation, visual
pose-estimation accuracy, robustness to keypoint outliers, state
estimation, guidance and control, and end-to-end Monte Carlo
performance. The experiments are designed to first verify the individual
components and subsequently evaluate their interaction in the complete
closed-loop rendezvous system.

\subsection{Dynamics Validation}

The numerical implementation of the relative orbital dynamics was first
validated against the analytical HCW state-transition solution. The
comparison was performed over three orbital periods using the DOP853
integrator with relative and absolute tolerances of
$10^{-12}$ and $10^{-14}$, respectively. The resulting position and
velocity errors are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics_validation}.

The maximum position error between the analytical and numerical
solutions was $4.656\times10^{-10}$ m, corresponding to approximately
$0.47$ nm. The mean position error was
$1.816\times10^{-10}$ m. For velocity, the maximum error was
$5.494\times10^{-13}$ m/s, while the mean error was
$2.058\times10^{-13}$ m/s.

These errors are several orders of magnitude smaller than the
characteristic relative separation used in the rendezvous simulations.
Consequently, numerical propagation error is negligible compared with
the visual measurement and state-estimation uncertainties considered
in the subsequent experiments.

The tolerance convergence study in Fig.~\ref{fig:tolerance_convergence}
further demonstrates a monotonic reduction in both position and
velocity error as the integration tolerance is decreased. The results
confirm that the selected DOP853 configuration provides sufficient
numerical accuracy for the remainder of the simulation campaign.

\subsection{Vision-Based Pose Estimation}

The sensitivity of the visual pose-estimation pipeline to image-plane
measurement noise was evaluated using 200 Monte Carlo trials at each
pixel-noise level. Raw EPnP and Gauss--Newton-refined estimates were
compared in terms of translation and attitude error.

The results are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:pose_noise}. For the
lowest tested noise level of $0.10$ px, EPnP produced a translation
RMSE of $1.753$ cm and an attitude RMSE of $0.134^\circ$. The
Gauss--Newton refinement reduced these errors to $0.362$ cm and
$0.048^\circ$, respectively.

As the pixel noise increased to $5$ px, the translation error of the
raw EPnP solution increased to $84.725$ cm and the attitude error
increased to $6.032^\circ$. With Gauss--Newton refinement, the
corresponding errors were reduced to $20.245$ cm and $2.569^\circ$.

Thus, nonlinear refinement substantially improves the recovered pose
over the complete noise range tested. At $5$ px noise, for example,
the refinement reduces the translation error by approximately
$76.1\%$ and the attitude error by approximately $57.4\%$ relative to
the raw EPnP estimate.

All 200 trials were successfully solved at every tested noise level.
Therefore, the degradation observed with increasing pixel noise is
primarily an accuracy effect rather than a loss of solver convergence
within the tested conditions.
The Gauss--Newton refinement evaluated in this experiment is treated as
a separate pose-refinement study. It is not used in the end-to-end
runner, where robust pose estimation is performed using RANSAC--EPnP
with local EPnP inlier refitting.

\subsection{Robustness to Keypoint Outliers}

 We evaluate the robustness of pose estimation to erroneously matched visual correspondences. The outlier fraction was varied from $0$ to $30\%$ while the inlier pixel-noise level was held fixed at $\sigma_{\mathrm{px}}=1$ px. Two hundred independent trials were performed for each condition.

Figure \ref{fig:ransac_robustness} clearly highlights the impact of the lack of outliers in the bare EPnP design as compared to the RANSAC--EPnP design. Without a properly defined robustness scheme on correspondence inliers, the translation error for EPnP quickly jumped from $17.842$ cm for $0\%$ outliers to $815.754$ cm at $5\%$ and $940.195$ cm at $10\%$ outliers; furthermore, for $20\%$ and $30\%$ outlier percentages, the solver outright failed.

The trend of the attitude error was similarly dramatic. The EPnP attitude RMSE is reported at $1.392^\circ$ with $0\%$ outliers, $7.788^\circ$ at $5\%$ outliers, $13.580^\circ$ at $10\%$ outliers, and solver failure reached $96\%$ at $5\%$ outliers, $99\%$ at $10\%$ and $100\%$ failure at both $20\%$ and $30\%$ outliers.

Alternatively, all 200 of the tested trials in RANSAC--EPnP returned a feasible solution at every level of tested outlier corruption, and it performed acceptably in the range of $5.94$ and $7.82$ cm for translation error and maintained a $1.4^\circ$ attitude error throughout the entire study. These two figures represent strong proof of the necessity of robust correspondence selection in the proposed rendezvous architecture.

\subsection{State Estimation}

The MEKF and UKF were compared using identical direct Gaussian
position and attitude measurements in order to isolate the behavior of
the recursive state estimators from the visual pose-estimation
subsystem. The corresponding tracking errors are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:filter_tracking} and the numerical RMSE values are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:filter_results}.

Both filters produced nearly identical translational performance. The
position RMSE was $0.6018$ m for both the MEKF and UKF, while the
velocity RMSE was $0.0225$ m/s for both estimators. The maximum
position error was also identical at $1.5343$ m.

A small difference was observed in attitude estimation. The MEKF
achieved an attitude RMSE of $1.3346^\circ$, compared with
$1.3429^\circ$ for the UKF. The maximum attitude errors were
$7.0927^\circ$ and $7.6948^\circ$, respectively. Thus, the MEKF
provided a marginally lower attitude error for the tested trajectory.

Both estimators accepted 299 measurements, indicating that no
measurement rejection occurred under the controlled Gaussian
measurement model. The measured wall-clock execution time was
$1.922$ s for the MEKF and $0.965$ s for the UKF. Therefore, the UKF
implementation required approximately half the wall-clock time of the
MEKF for this particular experiment while producing comparable
position and velocity accuracy.

This experiment should be interpreted as a filter-isolation study.
The measurements are generated directly from the true state using the
specified Gaussian noise model and therefore do not contain the
range-dependent, potentially correlated error characteristics of the
EPnP visual measurements. The effect of the complete vision
measurement chain is evaluated separately in the end-to-end
experiment.

\subsection{Guidance and Control}

The guidance and control layer was evaluated by comparing LQR and MPC
for the same initial relative state and control constraints. The
resulting relative trajectories, control effort, and cumulative
propellant expenditure are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lqr_mpc_trajectory},
and the performance metrics are summarized in
Fig.~\ref{fig:lqr_mpc_comparison}.

Both controllers converged the initial 50-m along-track separation to
sub-millimetre final range within the 200-step horizon.
The total cumulative $\Delta v$ was $16.83$\,m/s for LQR and
$7.02$\,m/s for MPC, indicating that the receding-horizon optimizer
achieves approximately $58\%$ propellant savings over the fixed LQR
gain for this scenario. The maximum control magnitude was
$0.101$\,m/s$^2$ for LQR and $0.106$\,m/s$^2$ for MPC; the small
exceedance above $0.1$\,m/s$^2$ reflects that the per-axis box
constraint allows the Euclidean norm of the three-axis thrust vector
to exceed the nominal limit.

The principal trade-off lies in computational cost. LQR requires only
a matrix--vector multiply per step ($0.01$\,ms), whereas MPC solves
a quadratic program at each of the 200 steps ($3.00$\,ms per step),
corresponding to a $300\times$ increase in per-step computation.
For a 10-s control interval this latency is operationally acceptable,
but the difference becomes important on hardware with limited
on-board processing.

Overall, MPC provides a meaningful propellant advantage over LQR for
the unconstrained scenario tested here, at the cost of three orders of
magnitude greater computational load. The propellant advantage is
expected to grow when hard approach-cone constraints are added, since
MPC can explicitly plan constraint-satisfying trajectories whereas LQR
requires the constraint to be softened or pre-computed offline.

\subsection{End-to-End Monte Carlo Evaluation}

The complete vision-based rendezvous architecture was evaluated using
a Monte Carlo study with 25 trials. Three configurations were compared:
(i) perfect-state feedback, (ii) EKF with direct noisy measurements,
and (iii) the full vision--EKF pipeline. The simulation used 250
control steps, a maximum control acceleration of
$u_{\max}=0.3$ m/s$^2$, direct position-measurement noise of
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pos}}=0.5$ m, and pixel noise of
$\sigma_{\mathrm{px}}=1.5$ px for the vision configuration.

The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:montecarlo}. The perfect-state
and full vision--EKF configurations both converged to effectively zero
final range across the tested trials. In contrast, the direct EKF
configuration exhibited a substantially larger final-range
distribution, with a mean of approximately $0.23$ m and a spread
extending to approximately $0.55$ m.

The control-effort results reveal a corresponding difference between
the configurations. The perfect-state and full vision--EKF cases
required approximately $40.2$ m/s of cumulative $\Delta v$, whereas
the direct EKF configuration required approximately $68.2$ m/s.
Therefore, the direct noisy-measurement configuration consumed
approximately $70\%$ more propellant than the other two cases.

Most importantly, all three configurations achieved a docking success
rate of $100\%$ over the 25 Monte Carlo trials. Thus, under the tested
initial conditions, measurement noise, and control constraints, the
visual navigation pipeline did not reduce the observed docking
success rate.

The end-to-end result is particularly significant because the full
pipeline does not use the Gauss--Newton refinement evaluated in
Experiment B. Instead, visual measurements are processed through
RANSAC--EPnP with local inlier refitting, followed by MEKF state
estimation and LQR control. The observed $100\%$ success rate therefore
demonstrates successful closed-loop operation of the actual implemented
architecture under the tested conditions.

\subsection{Pixel-Noise Ablation}

The sensitivity of the complete vision--EKF rendezvous pipeline to
image-plane measurement noise was evaluated by varying
$\sigma_{\mathrm{px}}$ from $0.3$ px to $4.0$ px. Twenty Monte Carlo
trials were performed at each noise level. The resulting final range
and docking success rate are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_ablation}.

The mean final range remained very small throughout the experiment.
The mean values were approximately $2.0\times10^{-4}$ m at
$0.3$ px, $3.4\times10^{-4}$ m at $0.7$ px,
$4.4\times10^{-4}$ m at $1.0$ px, and
$4.2\times10^{-4}$ m at $1.5$ px. At larger noise levels, the mean
final range decreased to approximately $3.3\times10^{-4}$ m at
$2.0$ px and $1.1\times10^{-4}$ m at $3.0$ px. At the maximum tested
noise level of $4.0$ px, the final range converged to approximately
zero in the plotted metric.

The variation across trials, represented by the shaded $\pm1\sigma$
band, is larger than the change in the mean final range. Consequently,
the results do not indicate a monotonic degradation of terminal range
with increasing pixel noise over the tested range.

More importantly, the docking success rate remained at $100\%$ for
every tested pixel-noise level, including the maximum
$\sigma_{\mathrm{px}}=4.0$ px condition. Thus, the closed-loop
rendezvous controller maintained successful terminal convergence even
under substantially increased visual measurement noise.

This result should be interpreted within the tested simulation
conditions. In particular, the experiment does not establish
robustness to arbitrary image degradation, since the number and
geometry of the target landmarks, RANSAC threshold, maximum iteration
count, and controller constraints remain fixed. Nevertheless, the
experiment demonstrates substantial tolerance of the complete
navigation and control loop to the tested pixel-noise range.

\subsection{Overall Discussion}

The experimental results provide validation at both the subsystem and
system levels. The dynamics experiment established that numerical
propagation error is negligible relative to the other error sources,
with sub-nanometer position discrepancies and sub-picometer-per-second
velocity discrepancies relative to the analytical HCW solution.

The vision experiments demonstrated two complementary properties of
the pose-estimation subsystem. First, image-plane noise produces a
systematic increase in pose-estimation error, while explicit
Gauss--Newton refinement can substantially improve the raw EPnP
solution. Second, the outlier experiment demonstrated that robust
correspondence rejection is essential: bare EPnP failed in up to
$100\%$ of trials at sufficiently large outlier fractions, whereas
RANSAC--EPnP maintained successful pose recovery throughout the tested
range.

The state-estimation experiment showed that MEKF and UKF provide
similar translational accuracy under the controlled direct Gaussian
measurement model. The MEKF achieved a slightly lower attitude RMSE,
while the UKF required approximately half the wall-clock time of the
MEKF in the present implementation.

The guidance and control comparison showed that both LQR and MPC
converged to sub-millimetre final range. MPC achieved a $58\%$
reduction in cumulative $\Delta v$ ($7.02$ m/s vs.\ $16.83$ m/s for
LQR) but at a $300\times$ higher per-step computational cost
($3.00$ ms vs.\ $0.01$ ms). For the unconstrained scenario evaluated
here, LQR therefore offers an attractive computational advantage;
the propellant benefit of MPC becomes more pronounced when hard
approach-cone constraints must be enforced.

The end-to-end Monte Carlo experiment then demonstrated that the actual
vision--EKF pipeline successfully closed the rendezvous loop in all
25 trials. The full pipeline achieved $100\%$ docking success while
requiring approximately $40.2$ m/s of cumulative $\Delta v$, comparable
to the perfect-state reference and substantially below the
approximately $68.2$ m/s required by the direct noisy-measurement EKF
configuration.

Finally, the pixel-noise ablation showed that the full pipeline
maintained $100\%$ docking success for all tested pixel-noise levels
from $0.3$ px to $4.0$ px. Although the terminal-range variation
increased under some noise conditions, no loss of docking success was
observed within the tested simulation envelope.

Overall, the results indicate that the proposed architecture can
maintain closed-loop rendezvous performance despite uncertainty in
visual measurements. However, the results are simulation-based and
should therefore be interpreted as evidence of algorithmic robustness
rather than experimental flight validation. Real-camera imagery,
higher-fidelity spacecraft dynamics, illumination variation, partial
target visibility, and hardware-in-the-loop testing remain important
directions for future validation.
