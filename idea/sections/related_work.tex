\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews the four bodies of work the present study draws on ---
autonomous rendezvous and proximity operations, monocular relative pose
estimation, robust correspondence estimation, and constrained guidance and
control --- and then states, in Section~\ref{sec:gap}, the specific question
that the intersection of these literatures leaves open and that this paper
addresses.

\subsection{Autonomous Rendezvous and Proximity Operations}
\label{sec:rw_arpo}

Automated rendezvous and docking has a multi-decade heritage in human
spaceflight and is now a core enabling capability for on-orbit servicing,
active debris removal, and in-space assembly
\cite{fehse2003rendezvous,flores2014}.  The translational dynamics of
close-proximity operations are conventionally described by the
Hill--Clohessy--Wiltshire (HCW) linearisation, which admits a closed-form
state-transition solution for relative motion in the local orbital frame
about a near-circular reference orbit \cite{clohessy1960}.

What distinguishes the current generation of missions from that heritage is
that the target is \emph{non-cooperative}: it carries no docking aids, no
retro-reflectors, no telemetry link, and in general tumbles.  The
RemoveDEBRIS mission demonstrated net and harpoon capture of deployed
sub-satellites in low Earth orbit and, in doing so, exercised a
vision-based navigation chain against an uncontrolled target
\cite{aglietti2020removedebris}.  The AVANTI experiment on board BIROS
achieved autonomous far- and mid-range rendezvous with a non-cooperative
object using angles-only optical measurements alone
\cite{gaias2018avanti}.  Both demonstrations establish that the guidance
layer is mature; both also make clear that the binding constraint has moved
to the perception layer, and specifically to obtaining pose estimates of
adequate quality \emph{and adequate frequency} from a passive camera.

\subsection{Model-Based Monocular Relative Pose Estimation}
\label{sec:rw_pose}

Recovering the six-degree-of-freedom relative pose of a known target from a
single image is normally cast as a Perspective-$n$-Point (PnP) problem.
Kelsey~\textit{et al.}~\cite{kelsey2006} gave an early demonstration that
model-based visual pose estimation is viable for autonomous rendezvous and
docking; the reviews of Opromolla~\textit{et al.}~\cite{opromolla2017review}
and Pasqualetto Cassinis~\textit{et al.}~\cite{cassinis2019review} survey the
resulting family of monocular, stereo and LIDAR pose determination
architectures and their reported accuracies.

Among PnP solvers, the EPnP algorithm of Lepetit~\textit{et al.}
\cite{lepetit2009epnp} remains widely used onboard because of its
$\mathcal{O}(n)$ complexity: the reference points are written as a weighted
combination of four virtual control points, and the pose follows from a
compact eigen-decomposition, optionally relinearised and refined by a
Gauss--Newton step in the null-space coefficients.  Two properties of EPnP
matter for the present work and are treated explicitly in
Section~\ref{sec:epnp_gn}: its four-control-point construction degenerates
when the reference points are coplanar, a case Lepetit~\textit{et al.}
handle by dropping to three control points; and its accuracy without the
$\beta$ refinement step is materially worse than with it.

Subsequent solvers have improved on both the robustness and the optimality
of the algebraic solution.  Ke and Roumeliotis~\cite{ke2017ap3p} give a
direct algebraic P3P solution that avoids the intermediate depth
polynomial, and Terzakis and Lourakis~\cite{terzakis2020sqpnp} present
SQPnP, a sequential-quadratic-programming formulation that is globally
optimal for the least-squares PnP cost at a cost comparable to EPnP and
that does not degrade in the planar and quasi-degenerate configurations
where the control-point construction is ill-conditioned.  These solvers now
form the default non-iterative back ends in widely used vision libraries.
The present framework retains EPnP as its hypothesis generator, both because
its behaviour under the degeneracies above is instructive and because the
question this paper asks --- when is a pose solution \emph{available} ---
is largely independent of which non-iterative solver produces it; the
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:res_pose} against a reference implementation
quantifies how much accuracy that choice costs.

\subsection{Learning-Based Pose Estimation and Its Benchmarks}
\label{sec:rw_learned}

The most active line of work since 2018 replaces hand-designed feature
extraction with a learned front end.  Sharma~\textit{et al.}
\cite{sharma2018} first applied a convolutional network to non-cooperative
spacecraft pose estimation, and Sharma and D'Amico~\cite{sharma2020nn}
developed the Spacecraft Pose Network into a full monocular pose
estimation pipeline evaluated against synthetic and hardware-in-the-loop
imagery.  Proen{\c{c}}a and Gao~\cite{proenca2020urso} showed that
photorealistic rendering of orbital scenes is sufficient to train a pose
regressor, and released the URSO datasets to that end.

This line of work is unusually well benchmarked.  The Satellite Pose
Estimation Challenge established the SPEED dataset and a common metric, and
its results and competition design are reported by
Kisantal~\textit{et al.}~\cite{kisantal2020}; SPEED+
\cite{park2022speedplus} added hardware-in-the-loop and lightbox imagery
specifically to expose the synthetic-to-real domain gap, and the follow-on
competition results are analysed by Park~\textit{et al.}
\cite{park2023spec2021}.  Subsequent work has attacked the domain gap
directly through multi-task learning and online refinement
\cite{park2024spnv2} and, most recently, through architectures explicitly
constrained to be flight-ready, evaluated on robotic-testbed imagery while
trained only on synthetic renderings \cite{park2026spnv3}.  Reported
accuracies on these benchmarks are now at the level of a few tenths of a
degree in orientation and a fraction of a percent of range in position.

Three features of this literature bear directly on the present paper.
First, the benchmarks are \emph{open-loop}: a network is scored on a fixed
image set, and the camera trajectory that produced those images was not
generated by a controller acting on the network's own output.  Second, the
metrics are conditioned on a solution being produced --- images on which
detection fails are either excluded or absorbed into an aggregate score, so
the benchmark reports the accuracy of the estimates that exist rather than
the rate at which estimates exist at all.  Third, learned front ends require
labelled datasets and provide no geometric guarantee, which is why
model-based pipelines remain the reference point when the target geometry is
known; the present work follows the model-based route for exactly that
reason, and treats its front end as a source of \emph{intermittent}
measurements.

\subsection{Robust Correspondence Estimation}
\label{sec:rw_ransac}

Pose estimation degrades sharply when correspondences are wrong, which
under partial occlusion, specular glint, Earth-limb background or repeated
structure on the target is the normal case rather than the exception.
Random Sample Consensus \cite{fischler1981ransac} remains the standard
framework: minimal-sample hypotheses are scored by consensus-set size, and
the iteration budget can be adapted online from the running inlier ratio.
The locally optimised variant, LO-RANSAC \cite{chum2003loransac}, refits the
model on the full inlier set of each so-far-best hypothesis, which
substantially improves accuracy without increasing the number of random
hypotheses.

Two developments since then are relevant.  USAC \cite{raguram2013usac}
unifies the sampling, verification, degeneracy-detection and local-refinement
stages into a single framework and shows that the gains of the individual
variants are largely complementary.  MAGSAC++ \cite{barath2020magsacpp}
removes the inlier--outlier threshold altogether by marginalising over the
noise scale and applying an iteratively reweighted least-squares
optimisation, which makes the estimator markedly less sensitive to the
choice of the reprojection threshold --- historically the most
hand-tuned parameter of the whole pipeline.  The framework presented here
uses adaptive-termination RANSAC with EPnP as the hypothesis solver and a
final inlier refit on the $SO(3)$ manifold; the breakdown point measured in
Section~\ref{sec:res_ransac} is the quantity a threshold-free estimator
such as MAGSAC++ would be expected to improve.

\subsection{Recursive State Estimation for Tumbling Targets}
\label{sec:rw_filter}

Kalman-type recursive estimators are the standard means of fusing noisy pose
measurements with a relative-motion model.  Because attitude is
parameterised by unit quaternions, an additive extended Kalman filter cannot
preserve the norm constraint through the update, and the standard remedy is
the multiplicative EKF (MEKF), which propagates a small rotation-vector
error state composed multiplicatively with a reference quaternion
\cite{markley2003}.  The unscented Kalman filter \cite{julier2004} instead
propagates a deterministic set of sigma points and requires no Jacobians,
at the cost of more dynamics evaluations per step.  Both are implemented and
compared here under identical measurement conditions
(Section~\ref{sec:res_filter}), and, as Section~\ref{sec:mekf_residual}
sets out, the attitude residual is formed multiplicatively on the group
rather than as a difference of global rotation vectors --- a distinction
that is invisible for small errors and dominant near the $\pi$-radian
singularity that a tumbling target visits routinely.

The closest existing work to the present study's concern with coupling is
that of Pasqualetto Cassinis~\textit{et al.}~\cite{cassinis2021coupled},
who compare tightly- and loosely-coupled arrangements of a CNN feature
detector, a covariance-aware PnP solver and an MEKF, and show that
propagating the image-level covariance into the filter yields an
advantageous coupling between rotational and translational states.  That
study couples the \emph{uncertainty} of the pose solution to the filter.  It
does not couple the \emph{existence} of the pose solution to a controller,
because there is no controller in the loop: the trajectory is prescribed.

\subsection{Constrained Guidance and Control for Proximity Operations}
\label{sec:rw_control}

Linear quadratic regulation gives a closed-form infinite-horizon feedback
law from an offline solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation
\cite{anderson1990}, and under the HCW linearisation is cheap enough to be
uncontroversial onboard.  It cannot, however, express the constraints that
define a safe approach: a thrust bound, a keep-out zone, or an approach
corridor.  Model predictive control supplies exactly that by re-solving a
finite-horizon constrained program at every step \cite{mayne2000}.

The application of MPC to spacecraft rendezvous is by now a substantial
literature in its own right.  Di Cairano~\textit{et al.}
\cite{dicairano2012mpc} formulated the proximity-manoeuvring problem with
thrust bounds and line-of-sight constraints for both stationary and rotating
platforms, and showed that for the stationary case an explicit offline
solution removes the need for onboard optimisation entirely.  Breger and
How~\cite{breger2008} addressed the complementary safety question, generating
trajectories that remain passively safe under thruster failure.  More
recently, Specht~\textit{et al.}~\cite{specht2023tubempc} applied tube-based
MPC to autonomous rendezvous, using a robust invariant tube to guarantee
constraint satisfaction under bounded disturbance --- the standard modern
answer to the recursive-feasibility problem that a hard corridor constraint
creates.

The present work does not aim to improve on these formulations.  It
implements a receding-horizon controller with a second-order approach-cone
constraint and soft-constrained slack for recursive feasibility
(Section~\ref{sec:cone}), and evaluates it against LQR on the same scenario,
so that the guidance layer is representative of current practice while the
study's variable of interest remains the perception layer.  One point from
this literature is, however, load-bearing for the present argument: every
formulation above assumes the state estimate is \emph{available at the
control rate}.  The tube in \cite{specht2023tubempc} bounds a disturbance,
not a measurement outage; the explicit map in \cite{dicairano2012mpc} is a
function of a state that is assumed known.  Intermittent measurement is
outside the disturbance model these guarantees are built on.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Simulation Frameworks}
\label{sec:rw_sim}

Integrated testbeds exist to evaluate complete navigation and control chains
under controlled conditions.  Gasbarri~\textit{et al.}~\cite{testbed2014}
present a ground-based facility for vision-based formation flying, and the
hardware-in-the-loop imagery underlying SPEED+ and its successors
\cite{park2022speedplus,park2026spnv3} is produced by robotic testbeds of
the same kind.  Such facilities reproduce the photometric realism that
synthetic rendering struggles with.  What they typically do not reproduce is
closed-loop causality: the robot follows a commanded trajectory, so the
images the estimator sees do not depend on the estimates the controller
acted on.  The present work makes the opposite trade --- it forgoes
photometric realism, using an analytic keypoint front end with known
correspondences, in exchange for a fully closed causal loop in which the
camera's viewpoint at step $k+1$ is a consequence of the pose solution at
step $k$, and in which a lost measurement therefore propagates.

\subsection{Gap Addressed by This Work}
\label{sec:gap}

Taken together, the literature above characterises the two halves of a
vision-based rendezvous system very well, and characterises them separately.

On one side, the pose-estimation literature reports \emph{accuracy}:
rotation and translation error as a function of range, illumination,
architecture and domain, measured on fixed image sets
\cite{kisantal2020,park2023spec2021,park2024spnv2,park2026spnv3,cassinis2019review}.
These numbers are conditioned on a pose being returned.  On the other side,
the guidance and control literature reports \emph{closed-loop outcomes}:
propellant, terminal accuracy, constraint satisfaction, and --- in Monte
Carlo studies --- docking success rate, under the assumption that the state
estimate arrives at the control rate
\cite{dicairano2012mpc,breger2008,specht2023tubempc}.  Work that
deliberately bridges the two, such as \cite{cassinis2021coupled}, does so at
the level of \emph{covariance}: it asks how measurement uncertainty should
enter the filter, not how often a measurement enters at all.

The consequence is that a designer holding both halves of the literature can
predict neither of the two things they most need.  Given a measured pose
accuracy, there is no model that says whether the closed loop will close;
and given a closed-loop success rate, there is no attribution of that number
to the perception chain, because the two are not connected by any statement
about measurement \emph{availability}.  Availability is not a free parameter
of the perception system: it is a geometric consequence of the same
approach trajectory the controller is generating.  As the chaser closes, the
target's projected extent grows as $1/z$ until it exceeds the field of view,
at which point the keypoint set visible to the solver collapses --- and it
collapses precisely in the terminal phase, where the control authority
needed to recover is smallest.

This paper closes that gap.  Its contributions are, first, a closed-loop
simulation in which perception, estimation and control are causally coupled,
so that measurement dropout is generated by the trajectory rather than
imposed on it; second, a closed-form criterion for the range at which
field-of-view saturation begins to remove keypoints, $z_{\text{fill}} =
fL/W$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_dropout}), validated against the measured
dropout knee; and third, a dispersed Monte Carlo characterisation
(Section~\ref{sec:res_configs}) showing that closed-loop performance is
insensitive to pose accuracy across almost an order of magnitude of pixel
noise and then fails abruptly, with the failure attributable to availability
rather than accuracy.  The practical implication --- that the design margin
worth buying is field of view and keypoint redundancy at short range, not
sub-pixel feature localisation --- is not derivable from either half of the
literature alone.
